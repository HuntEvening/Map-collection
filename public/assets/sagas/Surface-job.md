She gripped a firm hold of the man's ash-filled hair, yanking his head back until his throat was bare, the knife pressed against his skin. She hesitated, not for long, but just enough for the man to notice and start his whimpering again, the last scraps of dignity spilling out in pleas for his life. His eyes rolled up, desperate to meet hers. Elithana felt a twist of something, pity and crushed it. A grown human male, reduced to this. Begging. Crying. Weak, men like him deserved to die. She didn't hesitate again. The blade moved in one smooth, practised stroke. Warm blood sprayed her hand as he gargled out one final, pitiful sound. She shoved his body away, watching it slump to the ground, as the blood crept through the gravel, forming a thin, dark puddle around her boots. For a moment, her stomach tightened. She told herself it was the smell: blood, smoke, and the acrid sting of burning wood. Nothing more.

Turning, she surveyed the rest of the scene. Her soldiers had rounded up the villagers, two ragged lines of kneeling figures, men separated from the women and children. The firelight from nearby homes bathed them in a wavering orange glow, their shadows stretching and writhing across the scorched ground. Screams cut through the air, high and piercing, clashing with the roar of burning buildings behind them. One man broke loose. He surged forward, slamming into one of the drow before pushing her away and sprinting toward what presumably would be his family. He didn't make it far. Two of her warriors tackled him, dragging him back and forcing him down onto his knees. One of them looked to her,  
*"vel'bol zhah dosst quarth venta'kyorl?"* - "What's your command, captain?" Elithana sighed and then coughed as the smoke clawed at her lungs. She rubbed her forehead while she thought, only to smear it with even more blood. For a moment, she stared at the dark stain on her fingers, then wiped them clean on a rag before she spoke.  
*"Uk zhah natha gareth s'lat'halin ji udos ssrig'luin ukta. Jhal usstan xuat ssinssrin ulu t'zaraw xuil mzild iblith maristo. Elgg l'dalhar lu't'yin l'j'nesst."* - "He's a strong fighter, so we keep him. But I don't want any more surface filth tonight. Kill the child. Then the woman." The warrior gave a small nod. 
*"Orn tlu, venta'kyorl."* - "Will be done, captain."

They sprang into action, dragging the woman out in front of her husband, prying the child from her arms. The child's shrill scream tore through the air, a raw, panicked sound that cut off in an instant as one of the drow drove a blade into the child's small chest. The mother screamed once before the blade cut her throat. All Elithana could see from where she stood was how the woman clutched her hands around her wound, as if she could hold her life in with her bare hands. Her eyes found her husband's, pleading, terrified, before she collapsed out of sight. Elithana did not look away. She watched, silent and unmoved, as the blood pooled around the man. The man thrashed against his restraints, screamed and sobbed, but none of the drow paid him any mind.

The rest of the drow grew tired of him and left him bound to a fence until he calmed down. Elithana walked over, planting her boot firmly on his hand to stop any sudden lunge, before crouching to meet his bloodshot eyes.  
"From now on," she said, her tone steady but low, "you'll have two options in life. Use that strong fighting spirit of yours, channel that rage within, and become something… or let this be your defeat. Choose the latter, and you'll suffer the same fate, if not worse." He glared at her through his tears, trembling but silent. Elithana stood, signalling to the others. They dragged him back to the line and secured him at the end with the other captives. Elithana cast one last look at the broken villagers. Over the ridge, dawn was spilling across the hills.  
*"Lest alu delmah!"* - "Let's go home!" she announced, and, without another glance at the dead or the burning village, Elithana took the lead, guiding her warband back toward the waiting caves.

---

Elithana led the column in silence, only the sound of birds singing at the coming of dawn and someone in the captive column weeping quietly. None dared raise their voices now. The captives shuffled in the middle of the drow raiding party, bound at the wrists and stripped of shoes to slow escape. Some limped, and some had fallen and been killed along the way. Slowly they made their way through the forest hills up towards the hidden tunnel entrance. They reached the clearing where the entrance was concealed behind a split rock face in the forest, only visible to those with a trained eye, covered in ivy and roots. It gaped open like a wound in the earth, barely wide enough for two to walk side by side. She stayed behind, keeping watch while each member of her party passed by, leading the slaves into the darkness. She looked out over the hillside, a pillar of smoke still visible where the village once stood. The only grave marker for the dead was ash and blood. She turned from the harsh brightness of day and entered the soothing darkness of the cave. It was cool, damp, and utterly devoid of birdsong. The surface faded behind them as they began the descent.

The light dwindled quickly. She passed by everyone in the group, working her way to the front of the party again. Each time she passed one of the captives, she studied them with a quick glance. She enjoyed seeing the fear in their eyes, or how completely lost they seemed in the absence of light. When she finally reached the front, she gave a quiet order and the drow lit lamps, small orbs of dull purple and cold blue light that clung to the tips of spears like threads on a spider’s web. It wasn't much light. The slaves would still struggle, but it was enough to keep them from stumbling over their feet again and again.

Slowly, the terrain changed under their feet: uneven, and sometimes slick with moss. All of it sloped steadily downwards, deeper and deeper. They passed narrow paths, and the occasional chasm, bridged by walkways made of woven spider silk. They walked in single file in places, the captives guided with prodding spears or shouted commands. The deeper they went, the more alive the tunnels became. Moisture clung to the walls, which had now changed from their crude grey colour to a sleek black and white speckled rock. Strange, tubular, bioluminescent fungi were scattered along the floor and in crevices of the walls, flickering slowly like a heartbeat. At one point, a pale centipede the size of a dog scuttled across the ceiling above them. The humans flinched, but the drow didn't even glance up. Elithana stayed near the front, guiding them through familiar territory. She had taken this path many times as a child. So many times that she could have walked this path blindfolded, and nearly did once, during training. Still, she caught herself glancing back now and then, not at her soldiers, but at the man she'd spared. His face was cast in shadow, but he held himself differently now. Hollowed, emptied.

After nearly a day of travel, with only two short breaks for water and rest, the passage finally widened again. A fork in the tunnel led to a higher ledge, and Elithana climbed it alone ahead of the others, pausing at its peak. Below her, the cavern opened in full: a vast hollow, like darkness embracing a glowing jewel. The city. Keanthas. The city stretched out over several pillars that connected to the ceiling. Each pillar was covered in elegant structures, layered with artistry. All were connected by bridges of webbed stone and obsidian, spanning the chasms in elegant arcs. Along the walls of the cavern were numerous walkways carved out, connecting the naturally formed tunnels to the various layers of the city. The whole city seemed to pulse with colour, mostly violet, but there were streaks of turquoise and silver, all from the bioluminescent mushrooms clustered along walls and walkways, carefully grown into chandelier-like shapes. But high above it all, sprawled out like a spider connecting its web to all pillars, hung the Temple of Endless Threads. Built with the finest obsidian, violet crystal, and stained-glass windows depicting Lolth's triumphs over rival gods, the place where the priestesses of Lolth chanted litanies over fresh sacrifices. Sacrifices she herself were about to deliver. It was beautiful. Elithana stared at it for a long time. The cold air of the Underdark pricked at her skin. She was home.

She heard footsteps behind her, and turned. One of her lieutenants nodded.  
"The captives are ready." Elithana nodded back, adjusting her gear before announcing,  
"Bring them through. Sort them by the pens. We’re taking the long path there, make sure they look worth the coin. Nobles will bid by dusk."

--- 

The tunnel spilled them out onto a high causeway overlooking Keanthas. Elithana slowed, letting the slaves feel the weight of it, the enormity, the strangeness, and the dread. Behind her, the procession continued in grim silence. Drow soldiers marched with perfect posture; showing themselves off was just as important as showing off their bounty. The captives, bruised and broken, were reminders of her strength. Each chained body was a currency. The noble tiers of Keanthas had already taken notice. Masked matrons and favoured sons looked down from their terraces, their expressions unreadable beneath embroidered veils. Some watched with predatory interest, others with veiled contempt.
*"Let them watch,"* Elithana thought, catching one matron’s glance. *"Let them see how few we lost, how many we took."* Among the nobility, perception was as significant as power. Success had to be visible. Failure punished. And weakness? It had to be hidden at any cost.

The path led them into the lower parts of the city, passing by the open promenade that wound along the merchant tier. From nearby towers, clerks, and scribes emerged with parchment and stone tablets, drow of lower houses, but still high enough to deal in flesh. All of them joined in the march. The air grew colder the deeper they descended, until they stood before the pens. Guards patrolled alongside driders around the half-dug and half-fenced-in accommodations for the slaves. The lighting around the area was minimal, some even pitch-black, particularly where surface-born slaves were kept, partly as cruelty, partly as control. The air here was thick with brine and iron, the smell of old blood mingling with the faint trace of ash. One of the scribes approached, flanked by two armoured handlers. He bowed slightly to Elithana, just enough to acknowledge rank.  
“How many for market?” he asked, gaze sliding from her to the captives.  
“Around twenty, depending on what the current need is,” she answered. “There are at least nine suitable for labour. Five for the pits. Three for potentially something more.”  
“Any claimed?” he asked, while going down the line, studying each one of them. She paused, then gestured toward the man, the one who had fought to reach his family. His face was hollow, drained. But his eyes were still burning. Still watching, following her every step.  
“That one,” she said. “Mine.” There was a flicker of unease from the scribe, and with a glance up to her, he asked:
“For House Amakir or personal stock?”  
“Personal,” she said coolly. The scribe studied her, raised an eyebrow, and without protest, reached into his satchel. He took a strip of tough parchment and pressed a carved brand against it, inked with her crest, a flower made of fractured gem-petal design, sharp as broken glass. Not the mark of a trader. Not of a priestess. Not even the full sigil of House Amakir. It was her personal mark. A statement: this one is claimed by a rising daughter, not yet matron, but not to be questioned. He tied the parchment to the man’s wrist, muttering a spell under his breath. The mark flared faintly with dull green light, disappearing into the skin. Without further comment, he turned and began sorting the rest of the captives, snapping orders to handlers, marking ages and conditions, recommending fates: breeding pens, blood arenas, manual labour, or immediate sacrifice. She didn't stay to watch. She had seen it too many times to pretend it mattered. Instead, she stepped aside to observe the man now marked as hers. He was breathing hard, gaze fixed on the glowing wrist. Part horror, part confusion. He looked up at her. She waved the guards over, then turned on her heel, walking away as the man was shoved into the pens with the others.
